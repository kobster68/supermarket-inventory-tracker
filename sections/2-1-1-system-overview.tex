\subsubsection{System Overview}
\label{sec:system-overview}

The system tracks the inventory of a regional supermarket chain. The chain has three
stores and one distribution center (\DC) today, and it will grow to ten stores. Each
store has a store system, and the \DC has a central system. A wide-area network (WAN)
link connects each store system to the central system. This link is part of our system.

Inventory changes start when a cashier scans a product that a shopper buys. Each scan at
the point-of-sale (POS) system makes a sales event, and a store makes up to 100 events
per second. Each sales event decreases the store inventory. When the store inventory of a
product falls to its minimum stock quantity, the store system creates a shipping order to
the \DC. The store manager confirms the order, and the \DC fulfils it by shipping more
product to the store. Thus, each shipping order starts at a store.

We assume that the \DC always has enough stock to satisfy each shipping order. How product
reaches the \DC is outside the scope of this system.

The store system keeps the store inventory, so that the store manager can read and change
it when the link to the \DC is down. The central system keeps the master record of the
stock in every store and in the \DC. The central system also sends the store events to a
variable number of other clients. An external analytics client is a future client of this
type.

Products belong to product families, and a family can contain other families to any
depth. A product may belong to more than one family, and the family-value report counts it
once per family it is in. Managers ask for three types of report: the lowest-stock items,
the overstocked items, and the total value of a product family.
